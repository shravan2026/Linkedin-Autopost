Let's talk about ghost-writing in pharma — the elephant in the room that most medical writers won't discuss publicly.

After 15 years in this industry, I have opinions. Strong ones.

First, let's define what we're talking about:

Ghost-writing = When a medical writer drafts a publication (journal article, review, abstract) but receives NO authorship credit, and the named authors may have had minimal involvement in the writing.

The scale of the problem:
→ Estimates suggest 40-75% of industry-sponsored publications involve professional writers
→ ICMJE guidelines clearly state: substantial contributions to writing = authorship eligibility
→ Yet the industry standard remains: writers get acknowledged (maybe) but not credited as authors

My honest take:

1. LEGITIMATE medical writing support is NOT ghost-writing. When writers draft based on author input, with full author review and approval — that's professional collaboration. Authors own the content.

2. The problem arises when "authors" rubber-stamp papers they didn't meaningfully contribute to. When KOLs lend their name for honoraria without intellectual engagement. THAT is unethical.

3. Transparency is the solution — not eliminating professional writers. ICMJE guidelines, GPP3 (Good Publication Practice), and CONSORT already provide frameworks.

4. The real question: Why does the industry resist giving writers proper credit? Because acknowledging writer involvement is perceived as diminishing the "author's" credibility.

What I practice and advocate:
→ Full disclosure of writing support in publications
→ Meaningful author engagement at every draft stage
→ Writers acknowledged by name in the acknowledgments section at minimum
→ Following ICMJE criteria strictly for authorship decisions

The profession has matured enormously. But we still have work to do on transparency.

Where do you draw the line between writing support and ghost-writing?
