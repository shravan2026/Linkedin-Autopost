CRISPR got FDA approval — and nobody is talking about the regulatory documentation nightmare that made it possible.

In December 2023, FDA approved Casgevy (exagamglogene autotemcel) — the world's first CRISPR-based gene therapy for sickle cell disease. Vertex Pharmaceuticals and CRISPR Therapeutics made history.

But here's what the headlines missed:

The regulatory submission for a gene editing therapy is unlike ANYTHING the pharma industry has seen before.

Why? Because traditional documentation frameworks weren't built for this:

→ No established long-term safety templates for permanent genomic modifications
→ Manufacturing documentation for autologous cell therapies requires patient-specific batch records
→ Risk-benefit narratives must address off-target editing effects with limited long-term data
→ The CMC (Chemistry, Manufacturing, Controls) section alone is 3-4x more complex than a small molecule
→ Follow-up commitments: FDA mandated 15-year post-approval monitoring

For medical writers, this is a paradigm shift:

1. You're writing about a therapy that permanently alters a patient's DNA. The language of uncertainty, benefit-risk, and informed consent must be extraordinarily precise.

2. Regulatory agencies across markets (FDA, EMA, PMDA) have different frameworks for gene therapies — harmonization is minimal.

3. The CTD Module 3 (Quality) for gene therapies is a beast. Vector characterization, potency assays, comparability studies — all require specialized writing expertise.

As more CRISPR therapies enter Phase 3 (think Intellia, Editas, Beam Therapeutics), the demand for writers who understand gene editing documentation will explode.

This isn't just another drug approval. It's a new category of medicine requiring a new category of writer.

Are you preparing for the gene therapy documentation wave?
