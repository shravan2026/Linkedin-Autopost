## Informed Consent Forms — Protecting Patients Through Clear Language

In clinical research, the Informed Consent Form is where science meets ethics. It is the document that empowers patients to make a voluntary, informed decision about participating in a trial. And writing it well is one of the most important responsibilities a medical writer carries.

The challenge? You are translating complex scientific procedures, potential risks, and study commitments into language that a person without a medical background can understand — without sacrificing accuracy or regulatory compliance.

This is harder than it sounds. Consider explaining a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial design to a patient who has never encountered clinical research. Or describing potential adverse events in a way that is honest but not unnecessarily alarming.

Principles I follow when writing ICFs:

- Use plain language. If a term requires a medical degree to understand, rewrite it.
- Structure information logically — what the study involves, what risks exist, what alternatives are available, and what rights the patient retains.
- Ensure the document meets IRB/IEC requirements and local regulatory standards.
- Remember that this is not a legal shield for the sponsor — it is a communication tool for the patient.

A well-written ICF builds trust. It respects the patient's autonomy and intelligence. It acknowledges that participating in a clinical trial is a significant personal decision.

Every time I write one, I remind myself: a real person will read this and decide whether to trust us with their health.
