Statistical Concepts Every Medical Writer Should Understand

You don't need to be a statistician. But you do need to understand what the statistics mean and how to present them accurately.

Over 15 years of writing CSRs, protocols, and clinical summaries, here are the statistical concepts I use most:

📈 p-values and confidence intervals – Know the difference between statistical significance and clinical significance. A p-value of 0.04 doesn't always mean the drug works meaningfully.

📈 Intent-to-treat vs per-protocol populations – Understanding why these give different results is critical for writing efficacy sections accurately.

📈 Multiplicity adjustments – When a study has multiple endpoints, you must understand how alpha is controlled. Misrepresenting hierarchical testing can mislead regulators.

📈 Hazard ratios and odds ratios – Essential for oncology and cardiovascular writing. Know how to interpret and present them with appropriate context.

📈 Missing data handling – LOCF, MMRM, multiple imputation — each approach has implications for how results are interpreted and presented.

📈 Subgroup analyses – Understand why these are typically exploratory and how to frame them without overstating findings.

📈 Non-inferiority margins – Writing non-inferiority study results requires precise language around margins and what "success" means.

My recommendation: take a short biostatistics course early in your career. Ask your statistician colleagues questions. Read the SAP before you write the CSR.

The best medical writers speak statistics fluently — not as experts, but as informed translators.
